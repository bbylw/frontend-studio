Walking, slowly

Four essays on what happens to attention when a journey stops being a way to arrive and becomes the thing itself.

Start with issue twelve

In this issue

  1. The long way to the coast

    Marta Vey walks the same ninety kilometres every March and finds a different road each time. A study of how repetition changes a landscape.

  2. Notes on a borrowed dog

    Ivan Brod takes a neighbour's lurcher up the same hill for a fortnight and discovers that the animal sets a pace no human would choose.

  3. What the stile knows

    A short history of the gaps built into English field boundaries, and what they assume about the people who cross them.

  4. Night walking in a small town

    Between two and four in the morning, a town of nine thousand people belongs to whoever is awake. An essay in nine streets.

From the editor

This issue began with a complaint. A friend had bought a watch that counted his steps and told him, at the end of every day, how far short of ten thousand he had fallen. He walked more, and enjoyed it less.

The four essays here are not against walking quickly, or against measuring. They are about the other thing: the walk that has no number attached to it, the detour taken because a lane looked interesting, the hour spent sitting on a wall because the light was doing something worth watching. Each writer was asked to keep going until the walk stopped being a means and became the subject.

We have kept the page plain for the same reason. There is nothing here but the words and the order they arrive in.

— The editor, Fieldnotes
